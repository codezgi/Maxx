Fixed- vs Adjustable-Loop Cortical Buttons: A Literature Summary

  1. Home
  2. Blog
  3. Fixed- vs Adjustable-Loop Cortical Buttons: A Literature Summary

Maxx Global Medical · 3 November 2026 · Last updated: 3 November 2026

Cortical suspension buttons are devices used for femoral graft fixation in anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) reconstruction. This article summarises what two reviews comparing fixed- and adjustable-loop designs found, and where the evidence stops.

In short: Biomechanical studies report differences between devices, but these are not uniform across all adjustable-loop devices. A 2022 clinical review found no difference in revision, knee laxity or patient-reported outcomes, while rating the certainty of evidence as very low.

How the two designs differ

In fixed-loop devices the loop length is predetermined. In adjustable-loop devices the loop is shortened after the button is deployed, drawing the graft into the socket. The central question in the literature is whether the adjustable mechanism lengthens more under cyclic load, and whether this translates to clinical outcomes.

Biomechanical findings

A 2018 meta-analysis of six biomechanical studies reported significant differences between the compared devices in ultimate load and cyclic displacement. The least cyclic displacement was seen with the fixed-loop device studied, while ultimate load also differed between the two adjustable-loop devices.[2] The findings therefore do not give a single answer for “all adjustable devices”.

These results come from porcine femur and bovine tendon or isolated-device models. Heterogeneity was very high (I² about 91–98%) and test protocols varied between studies. The authors call for clinical trials.[2]

Clinical findings

A 2022 systematic review and meta-analysis covers 15 cohort studies (2,686 patients) using hamstring autografts.[1]

RevisionNo pooled estimate calculated5 studies; high heterogeneity
Knee laxity (KT-1000, 2 years)Mean difference −0.15 mm (95% CI −0.54 to 0.24)4 studies, 357 patients; I² = 61%
Lysholm and IKDCNo meaningful difference6 and 5 studies

The authors conclude that both device types appear safe for ACL reconstruction, but rate the certainty of evidence as very low because of observational designs, risk of bias and heterogeneity, and call for better-quality studies.[1]

What this evidence does not say

  • It does not show that one device type is superior to the other.
  • Time-zero laboratory values do not replace clinical outcomes.
  • It cannot be directly generalised beyond hamstring autografts.
